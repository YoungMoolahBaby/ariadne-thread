# Ariadne's Thread

*Finding your way back out of immersive simulations.*

Theseus went into the labyrinth carrying a ball of thread that Ariadne gave him. He tied one end at the entrance and unwound it as he walked. When he had killed the Minotaur, he followed the thread back out. The old English word for a ball of thread is *clew*, and it is where our word *clue* comes from.

This book is about building that thread for simulations.

## The idea

Getting out of today's virtual reality is easy: you take the headset off. But every year the simulations get better, and each improvement removes one of the small signs that told you where you were: the weight on your face, the blurry edge of the view, the lag, the cable. As they keep improving, "getting out" turns into a real problem with several parts:

- getting your **body** back to the spot it started from,
- knowing **which reality** you are in, especially when simulations nest inside each other,
- coming back as **yourself**, with your usual sense of body, mood and values,
- knowing **how much time** has passed, and what happened outside meanwhile.

The idea is a "find my way back" thread: something laid down as you go in, tied to where you started, that you can follow back out. It should work when you are confused, when the simulation is very good, and (in the strongest version) when the simulation does not want you to leave.

## Two old stories hold most of the design

**Theseus's thread** worked because of six properties, and every design in this book is measured against them:

1. It was **tied outside** the labyrinth.
2. It was **continuous**. There were no gaps to lose it in.
3. The labyrinth **could not change it**.
4. He could follow it **by touch, in the dark**, without understanding the maze.
5. Its length told him **how deep** he was.
6. **Someone was waiting** at the other end.

**Hansel and Gretel** add the warning. The first time their parents led them into the forest, Hansel dropped white pebbles, and the children followed them home by moonlight. The second time he only had bread, and the birds ate it.

> **Pebbles, not bread.** A trail made of the world's own material can be eaten by the world. A breadcrumb trail stored inside a simulation is only as trustworthy as the simulation.

This is the most important rule in the book, and most of the hard problems follow from it.

## How the book is organised

- **[The problem](problem/starting-points.md)**: what "where you started" means, why better simulations make it harder, and the threats a thread has to survive, from simple drift to deliberate deception.
- **[What research already knows](research/exiting-vr.md)**: the academic work on leaving VR, staying aware of the real room, aftereffects such as dissociation, memory and time, wayfinding, dreams, ethics, and what headsets ship today.
- **[Lessons from elsewhere](elsewhere/real-ropes.md)**: fields that already solved versions of this: cave divers, firefighters, homing ants, computer security, anaesthesia and intensive care, myth and fiction, philosophy.
- **[Brainstorm](brainstorm/requirements.md)**: requirements for a good thread, a catalogue of ideas, a comparison, prototypes that could be built today, and open questions.
