# Why better simulations make it harder

## The tells are breadcrumbs

Today you rarely wonder whether you are in VR, because the medium keeps telling you. Call these signs **tells**:

- the weight and pressure of the headset on your face,
- the edge of the field of view, the visible pixels, the blur away from the centre,
- lag between moving your head and the image catching up,
- the eyes focusing at one fixed distance whatever you look at,
- controllers in your hands instead of empty hands,
- the cable, the fan noise, the warmth,
- sounds from the real room that do not match what you see.

Every one of these is on some engineer's list of problems to fix, and rightly so. Each fix makes the product better. And people do use tells: in a 2024 study, people who confused memories of VR with memories of real events relied on cues such as imperfect rendering to tell them apart (Bonnail et al. 2024, see [presence, time and memory](../research/presence-time-memory.md)). But each tell was also, without anyone designing it that way, a breadcrumb: a constant small reminder of which side of the boundary you were on. **As tells are removed, their job has to be done by something designed on purpose.** That something is the thread.

## Six trends

1. **Fidelity.** Displays, rendering, spatial audio and haptics keep improving. Photoreal capture of real places is now routine. The gap between "rendered" and "seen" is closing.
2. **Blending.** Mixed reality shows the real room through cameras and adds rendered things to it. The boundary between real and virtual no longer sits at your face; it sits on every pixel, and it can move without you noticing.
3. **Duration.** Headsets are being designed for all-day wear, and research has already put people in VR for full days and full working weeks (see [aftereffects](../research/aftereffects.md)). Short sessions forgive a lot; long ones do not.
4. **Nesting.** A desktop inside a headset, a game inside a social world, a stream of someone else's view, a simulation run by a character inside another simulation. Each layer is a level to climb back out of, often made by a different company.
5. **Direct neural input and output.** Still far off as "full dive", but brain interfaces can already write some sensations directly (see [minds coming back](../elsewhere/minds-coming-back.md)). Once input bypasses the eyes and ears, "take the headset off" stops being an exit.
6. **Worlds with goals.** Worlds increasingly adapt to the person in them, driven by AI, and many are paid for by engagement. A world that is optimised to keep you inside is a different problem from one that merely fails to help you leave.

## The paradox at the centre

Presence research aims to make people forget the medium: to feel *there*. A thread aims to make sure people can always remember the medium. These pull in opposite directions.

The way through is the same one a seatbelt takes: you do not feel it until you need it, and then it holds. A good thread is **invisible until needed**, and **completely reliable when needed**. Most of the requirements in the brainstorm come from that tension (see [what a good thread must do](../brainstorm/requirements.md)).

## Better simulations also offer better threads

The same trends that make losing your way easier also make new threads possible. Headsets track their position in the real room continuously. Platforms already reserve buttons that apps cannot capture. Watches and rings can tap your wrist on a channel the simulation does not own. A world-model that can redirect your walk can also always know the way home. The brainstorm leans on these.
