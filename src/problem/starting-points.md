# What "where you started" means

"Get back to where you started" sounds like one thing. It is at least five, and a thread can bring you back on one while leaving you lost on another.

| Starting point | What you lose | Today | Later |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Place** | Where your body is, which way it faces, what is around it | You lift the headset and you are facing the wall, not the desk | Redirected walking has quietly turned you around; you are in a room you never saw with real eyes |
| **Layer** | Which reality you are in, and how many levels deep | Rarely a problem: there is one level, and you know you are in it | A game inside a social app inside a passthrough room; photoreal passthrough you cannot tell from rendering |
| **Self** | Your usual sense of your body, mood, values and identity | A "floaty", unreal feeling for a while after a long session | Weeks in a different body, voice or personality; slow drift you do not notice |
| **Time** | How long you were in, what time it is now | "That was three hours?" | Time running at a different rate inside; days lost |
| **State** | What happened outside while you were in, and what you left running inside | Missed messages; the stove still on | Commitments, relationships and decisions made by you, or for you, while you were away |

## Place

Your body never leaves the room. But your sense of where it is does. Room-scale VR already moves you around a real space while showing you a different one, and some techniques deliberately make you walk a path that differs from the one you see (see [wayfinding](../research/wayfinding.md)). Coming out means re-attaching your sense of place to the real room: where the door is, where the desk is, which way you face.

## Layer

This is the one that gets interesting as simulations improve. Today the layer question has an easy answer because the boundary sits on your face. When the boundary moves into the pixels (mixed reality that blends the real room with rendered objects) or into the brain (a hypothetical full-dive interface), "am I out?" stops being obvious. Nesting makes it worse: each level of simulation-inside-simulation is another level you have to climb back through, and fiction has explored the fake exit, the false "you're out now", for decades (see [myth and fiction](../elsewhere/fiction.md)).

## Self

Virtual bodies change how people feel and act, and long immersion can leave people feeling detached from themselves or their surroundings (see [aftereffects](../research/aftereffects.md)). "Where you started" includes who you were when you went in. A good thread brings back the person, not only the body.

## Time

People lose track of time in absorbing activities, and VR appears to make this worse (see [presence, time and memory](../research/presence-time-memory.md)). Fairy tales are full of this: the visitor who spends a night in fairyland and returns to find a hundred years gone. A thread should carry a clock that the simulation does not control.

## State

The world outside kept going. Coming back includes catching up: what happened, what you missed, what you left running. Inside the simulation there is state too: things you built, places you meant to return to. Part of what makes leaving hard is the fear of losing what is inside, which is why one idea in the brainstorm is simply [making leaving free](../brainstorm/ideas-platform.md#p10-leaving-is-free).

## Why the split matters

The designs in this book are judged on which of these five they restore. A guardian boundary restores *place* and nothing else. A clock restores *time*. Nothing on the market today deliberately restores *self*, and very little addresses *layer*, because until recently there was no need.
