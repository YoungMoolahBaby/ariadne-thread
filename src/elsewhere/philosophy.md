# Philosophy: the limits of any test

Philosophy has worried about whether we can know we are awake for as long as there has been philosophy. Its conclusions set the outer limits on what a thread can do.

## The arguments

- **Zhuangzi's butterfly** (China, around the 4th century BCE). Zhuangzi dreams he is a butterfly, wakes, and cannot be sure whether he is a man who dreamt of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being a man.
- **Descartes' dream argument and evil demon** (*Meditations on First Philosophy*, 1641). There are no certain marks by which waking can be told from dreaming, and a powerful deceiver could feed us a whole false world.
- **The brain in a vat** (Putnam, *Reason, Truth and History*, 1981). A modern version of the demon: a brain kept alive and fed signals by a computer. Putnam argues the sceptical sentence refutes itself; the image outlived the argument.
- **The experience machine** (Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974). Would you plug into a machine giving you any experiences you want, for life? Most people say no, which Nozick takes to show that we care about more than how things feel from inside. It is the captivation problem as a thought experiment.
- **The simulation argument** (Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", *Philosophical Quarterly*, 2003). If civilisations commonly run simulations of their ancestors, most minds like ours would be simulated.
- **Virtual realism** (Chalmers, *Reality+*, 2022). Virtual objects are real digital objects, and life in a virtual world can be a genuine, meaningful life. On this view, coming out is not leaving illusion for reality; it is travelling between real places.
- **The self-model** (Metzinger, *Being No One*, 2003; *The Ego Tunnel*, 2009). The brain already presents us with a model of the world and of ourselves, which we do not experience as a model. Every experience is in some sense a simulation, so "getting back" means returning to the default model, not escaping models.

## What follows for design

**1. No test you perform with your senses can prove you are out.** Anything you can see, hear or touch can, in principle, be simulated, including the outside world, the exit, the totem and the person you trust. Descartes' demon and *eXistenZ* make the same point. The goal of a thread is therefore not *proof*. It is to make faking the exit costly, to make drift unlikely, and to make the true exit always reachable.

**2. Against a perfect liar, only action works, not information.** If the simulation can show you anything, then anything that merely *tells* you where you are can be faked. What cannot be faked from inside is an action from outside: someone shaking you awake, a power interlock cutting the feed, *Inception*'s kick. The strongest threads **act on you**. The brainstorm calls this the *extract* job, alongside *guide* and *verify* (see [requirements](../brainstorm/requirements.md)).

**3. Your memory is part of the trust chain.** Every check compares what you see now with what you remember. A system that can change memory can defeat every check. Today that is far-fetched, but ordinary memory errors already confuse virtual and real events (see [presence, time and memory](../research/presence-time-memory.md)), so records kept *outside* your head matter even now.

**4. "Where you started" is a choice, not a fact.** If Chalmers is right, the virtual world is not unreal, and someone may reasonably choose to live much of their life there. A thread should not assume the physical world is the only legitimate home. It should return you to the place *you* designated as home, under terms *you* set, in your usual frame of mind, before going in. The thread serves your choice; it does not override it.

**5. The self-model is the deepest layer.** Even with no headset at all, people sometimes feel unreal, detached or not themselves. Clinical work on depersonalisation (see [aftereffects](../research/aftereffects.md)) is, in a sense, research on the last level of the way back.
