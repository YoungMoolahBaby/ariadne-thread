# Myth and fiction

Stories have explored getting lost in other worlds for thousands of years. They are not evidence, but they are an excellent catalogue of failure modes, and several of them already contain a design.

## Myth and folklore

| Story | What happens | Lesson for a thread |
|---|---|---|
| **Theseus and Ariadne** | A thread tied at the entrance, unwound through the labyrinth, followed back out | The template: anchored outside, continuous, followable blind, measures depth |
| **Hansel and Gretel** (Grimm, 1812) | Pebbles shine in the moonlight and lead them home; the bread trail is eaten by birds | *Pebbles, not bread*: a trail made of the world's material can be consumed by the world |
| **Orpheus and Eurydice** | He may lead her out of the underworld only if he does not look back; he looks, and loses her | The way out can have rules, and a rule broken at the last step undoes everything. Exits must be robust to a single mistake |
| **Persephone** | Having eaten pomegranate seeds in the underworld, she is bound to return there for part of each year | Captivation: taking something from the world binds you to it |
| **Fairy food and fairy time** (many folk traditions) | Eat the food of fairyland and you cannot leave; a night there is years outside | Captivation and time distortion, the two quiet dangers |
| **Urashima Tarō** (Japan) | A fisherman spends what feels like days in an undersea palace, returns to find centuries gone, opens the box he was told not to open and ages at once | Time anchor: you need a clock the other world does not control |
| **Rip Van Winkle** (Irving, 1819) | Sleeps twenty years in the mountains, returns to a changed world | Coming back includes catching up on *state* |
| **Plato's cave** (*Republic*, book VII) | The freed prisoner, returning to the cave from the sunlight, cannot see in the dark and is mocked | Re-entry has its own adaptation period, in both directions |

## Film, television and novels

| Work | The exit problem | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| ***Simulacron-3*** (Galouye, 1964); ***World on a Wire*** (Fassbinder, 1973); ***The Thirteenth Floor*** (1999) | A simulated city whose inhabitants build their own simulation; the hero learns his world is also simulated | Nesting: the layer you think is the top may not be |
| ***Star Trek: The Next Generation***, holodeck | "Computer, arch" summons a doorway and controls; "end program" ends it | A reserved command that always works is the cleanest exit design in fiction |
| *TNG*, "Ship in a Bottle" (1993) | A holodeck character traps the crew inside a simulation of their own ship. They work out they never left, then trap him in a simulation in turn | The fake exit; and the fact that a convincing "outside" can be simulated too |
| ***The Matrix*** (1999) | Exits are tied to physical "hardline" telephones; an operator outside watches and guides | Exits as fixed infrastructure, and a person outside holding the other end |
| ***eXistenZ*** (1999) | After several levels of game-inside-game, the final line asks: "Are we still in the game?" | When exits nest, every exit is suspect |
| ***Open Your Eyes*** (1997) / ***Vanilla Sky*** (2001) | A man living in a commissioned lucid dream finds it turning into a nightmare; a "tech support" figure explains, and the way out is a leap | The contract signed before going in; and someone from outside who can step in |
| ***The Truman Show*** (1998) | A man whose whole life is a television set sails to the edge of the dome and finds a staircase and a door marked "Exit" | The exit exists physically, even when the world is built to hide it |
| ***Inception*** (2010) | Dreams within dreams; each person carries a **totem** whose exact behaviour only they know; a **kick** (a fall) wakes the dreamer, and music played outside warns that the kick is coming; the deepest level, limbo, makes people forget they are dreaming | Nearly a full design: personal invariants to check the layer, a physical extraction that works whatever you believe, a warning signal from outside, time that runs faster at depth. The architect who designs the levels is named Ariadne |
| ***Permutation City*** (Egan, 1994) | Copies of people run in simulations, some unable to leave | The self that comes back may not be the self that went in |
| ***Sword Art Online*** (novel 2009, anime 2012) | Players of a full-dive game discover the logout button is missing from the menu | The exit cannot live only inside the app's own menu |
| ***Black Mirror***, "Playtest" (2016) | A horror-game tester goes through several fake exits; the real cause of the disaster is a phone call that interferes with the device | Signals from outside can save or break you; the outside channel must be designed, not accidental |
| ***Black Mirror***, "San Junipero" (2016) and "Striking Vipers" (2019) | Worlds so wanted that leaving, or coming back to the real one, becomes the question | Sometimes the problem is not that you cannot leave, but that you do not want to |

## What fiction already designed

Pulling the recurring devices together:

- **The reserved command** (holodeck arch): always available, impossible for the program to hide.
- **The totem** (*Inception*): a personal check whose answer the world does not know.
- **The kick** (*Inception*): extraction that acts on the body, not on belief.
- **The warning signal** (*Inception*'s music, *The Matrix*'s operator): someone outside telling you it is time.
- **The fixed exit** (*The Matrix*'s phones, *The Truman Show*'s door): exits as infrastructure, not as a menu item.
- **The contract** (*Open Your Eyes*): terms set before you go in.

And the recurring failure: **the fake exit**, from "Ship in a Bottle" to *eXistenZ* to "Playtest". Fiction's consistent answer is that a convincing outside can always be simulated, so no exit you can only *see* is fully trustworthy. The [philosophy chapter](philosophy.md) takes that seriously.
