# Minds coming back

Medicine, therapy and ritual all bring people back from altered states: delirium, deep absorption, sensory deprivation, psychedelics, trance, spaceflight. Each has developed its own return protocol.

## Intensive care: reorientation

Delirium is common in intensive care, and the ICU Liberation bundle ("ABCDEF") was built partly to prevent it: **A**ssess and manage pain; **B**oth spontaneous awakening and breathing trials; **C**hoice of sedation; **D**elirium assessment and management; **E**arly mobility; **F**amily engagement. Its non-drug care for delirium includes **repeated reorientation**, glasses and hearing aids, cognitive stimulation, sleep protection and early mobilisation. In over 15,000 adults, doing more of the bundle was associated with better survival and less delirium (Pun et al. 2019). The PADIS guideline (Devlin et al. 2018) likewise recommends multi-part non-drug care.

The **Hospital Elder Life Program** (Inouye et al. 1999) cut delirium in older hospital patients from 15.0% to 9.9% with six protocols, one of them orientation, using an **orientation board** with the care team's names and the day's schedule.

Older still, **Reality Orientation** (Taulbee & Folsom 1966) surrounded confused patients with signs, clocks, calendars and orientation boards. Its benefits were mostly limited to orientation itself and often did not last.

## Ritual: the drum calls you back

In the shamanic drum journey that Michael Harner brought into print (*The Way of the Shaman*, 1980), a steady drum rhythm carries the journey, and the Foundation for Shamanic Studies' recordings **"conclude with a fast callback rhythm to bring the journeyer back to ordinary reality."** A signal from outside, agreed in advance, distinct from everything else, meaning only "come back".

## Psychedelic research: the sitter

Safety guidelines for psychedelic research (**Johnson, Richards & Griffiths 2008**) require at least two monitors, one **always present in the room**. Hand-holding is offered as **"interpersonal grounding"**, and the participant is released afterwards into the care of a friend or family member. The guidelines warn that "talking down" a distressed participant with reality-defining techniques **"may be counterproductive"**.

The **MAPS MDMA therapy manual** (2010) adds an overnight stay with a trained attendant, a prearranged ride home the next morning, and an **integration session** the following day.

## Sensory deprivation: the exit in your own hand

In a modern flotation-tank trial (**Feinstein et al. 2018**), participants floated for up to an hour in dark, silent, skin-temperature water. They **could end the float at any time**, controlled their own light, and had an intercom to the experimenter. Each session ended with a debrief. This pass found no published re-entry or re-orientation protocol for flotation beyond this.

## Spaceflight: weeks to re-adapt

Astronauts returning to gravity re-adapt over weeks. Posture control returned to baseline in about four days and balance and mobility within about a month (Tays et al. 2021). Private astronauts on short missions have struggled to stand, walk and turn for hours after landing.

## Writing into the brain: where "full dive" actually is

Brain interfaces can now write a little sensation directly:

- **Vision.** Fernández et al. (2021) implanted a 96-electrode array in the visual cortex of a blind woman for six months; stimulating several electrodes together produced spots of light that let her identify some letters and object edges. The **Orion** cortical implant's six-person feasibility study (ended 2025) reported, in a company release, better detection of shapes and motion with the device on. Neuralink's **Blindsight** received an FDA Breakthrough Device designation in 2024, which is not approval; as of November 2026, no peer-reviewed human results were found.
- **Touch.** Microstimulation of the somatosensory cortex roughly halved the time a person took to complete a grasp-and-transfer task with a robotic arm (Flesher et al. 2021, one participant). Patterned stimulation has conveyed edges, orientation, curvature, motion and shape at the fingertips (Valle et al. 2025, two participants).

That is: tens to around a hundred electrodes, producing dots of light or patches of touch, in a handful of people. No study writes rich scenes, sound, smell, body position or whole-body touch. "Full dive" is far away. But the direction is real, and once input bypasses the eyes, "take off the headset" stops being an exit.

## Lessons for the thread

1. **Reorientation is a recognised clinical intervention.** Clocks, calendars, names and schedules work for orientation, even if modestly. The [airlock](../brainstorm/ideas-platform.md#p6-the-airlock) and [re-entry checklist](../brainstorm/ideas-you.md#y4-re-entry-checklist) are an orientation board for one.
2. **Someone in the room is the standard of care.** Every serious practice of deep alteration keeps a person present, offers touch as grounding, and hands the person over to someone familiar afterwards ([O1](../brainstorm/ideas-people.md#o1-the-anchor-person)).
3. **The call-back signal is an old technology.** The drum's callback rhythm is [O2](../brainstorm/ideas-people.md#o2-the-call-back-signal) in its original form.
4. **Do not argue someone out.** "Talking down" with reality-defining arguments can backfire. Grounding through touch and presence is preferred. Threads should *ground* rather than *persuade*: a hand, a heartbeat, real light, not an explanation of why this is real.
5. **The exit in your own hand.** The flotation trial gives control of the exit, the light and a line to the outside to the person inside: a working [exit contract](../brainstorm/ideas-platform.md#p1-the-exit-contract) and anchor person in one.
6. **Re-entry has a tail.** MAPS's next-morning integration session and the weeks astronauts need suggest coming back is not finished when the session ends. [Before and after (Y5)](../brainstorm/ideas-you.md#y5-before-and-after) should include a check the next day.
