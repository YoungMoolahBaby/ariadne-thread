# Aftereffects: dissociation and game transfer

Coming out of the headset is not the same as coming back. This chapter covers what lingers: in the senses, in the sense of self, and in behaviour.

## Dissociation

- **Aardema et al. (2010)**: after VR exposure (N=30), depersonalisation and derealisation rose and the sense of presence in *objective reality* fell. The rise was larger in people already high in dissociation or absorption.
- **Peckmann et al. (2022)**: a randomised trial (N=80) of playing *Skyrim* in VR or on a PC. VR raised depersonalisation and derealisation more, but the effect was mild, below clinical levels, and present only immediately after play.
- **Barreda-Ángeles & Hartmann (2023)**: a preregistered survey of 754 VR users. Symptoms were not linearly related to hours of use, and were more common in younger women and in people who felt strongly embodied in their avatars.
- **van Heugten-van der Kloet et al. (2018)**: a VR out-of-body set-up (N=25) raised acute dissociation, mainly depersonalisation.
- **Lavoie et al. (2021)**: VR raised absorption, which intensified negative emotions compared with a laptop; a follow-up found the emotion lingering as rumination hours later.

## The virtual self carries over

**Yee et al. (2009)**: the *Proteus effect*. The height of a person's avatar changed how they negotiated, and the effect carried into face-to-face negotiation afterwards. The self you bring back is partly shaped by the self you were inside.

## Long exposure

- **Steinicke & Bruder (2014)**: a 24-hour self-experiment in VR. The participant reported being confused about whether he was in the virtual or real world, and mixing up objects and events between the two.
- **Nordahl et al. (2019)**: two people spent 12 hours in consumer VR; sickness rose at about seven hours.
- **Biener et al. (2022)**: sixteen people worked in VR for a full week, eight hours a day. VR was worse than a normal office on most measures; two dropped out on the first day; there was some adaptation over the week.

## What lingers in the senses

- **Porter & Robb (2022)** collected about 1,700 Reddit comments and followed 20 new users for four weeks; 14 of the 20 reported lingering effects. Users **touched objects "to reaffirm they are indeed real"**, and tried to teleport or press controller buttons in real life. Effects mostly appeared after at least an hour in VR, and faded with regular use.
- **Gallagher (2026)** is an up-to-date narrative review of sensorimotor, eye-movement and cognitive aftereffects and their mechanisms.
- **Zielasko et al. (2025)** (preprint, not peer-reviewed) found stress hormone levels stayed raised, working memory fell, and some symptoms peaked up to 90 minutes after exposure, and argue for longer wash-out periods before driving.

## Game transfer phenomena

**Ortiz de Gortari & Griffiths (2014, 2016)** documented *game transfer phenomena*: after heavy play, game elements carry into everyday life. Real objects are misperceived, game images appear as pseudo-hallucinations, game sounds are heard, and game actions start automatically. Their survey covered 2,362 gamers.

## Simulator sickness: the oldest exit protocols

Flight simulators met these problems decades before consumer VR.

- **Kennedy et al. (1993)** created the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire, still the standard measure.
- **Baltzley et al. (1989)**, as summarised by Johnson (2005): of 742 simulator exposures, 45% produced symptoms; of those, 75% cleared within an hour and 8% lasted more than six hours.
- **Stanney & Kennedy (1998)**: after 15 to 45 minutes of VR, symptoms lasted the whole measurement window.
- **Johnson (2005)**, a US Army Research Institute review, recommends waiting **6 to 12 hours** between simulator and aircraft, "usually ... until the next day", with up to 24 hours for severe cases, and **avoiding high-risk activities such as flying, driving, climbing, cycling and diving for at least 12 hours**. It also prescribes an exit ritual: the instructor should **turn off the visual display and turn on the cabin lights before asking the pilot to exit**.

That last instruction is the oldest airlock in the literature: end the simulated view, restore the real light, *then* move the body.

## What this means for the thread

1. **"Self" is a real starting point to return to.** Dissociation after VR is measurable, mostly mild and short-lived, and stronger in some people than others. A thread should restore it, and the [before-and-after](../brainstorm/ideas-you.md#y5-before-and-after) check is how you would notice.
2. **Users already invent reality checks.** Porter & Robb's users touch things to confirm they are real. That is a folk [Y2](../brainstorm/ideas-you.md#y2-reality-check-habits), and a sign the need is felt.
3. **Habit is a carry-over risk.** Trying to teleport or press a button in real life is the body still running the virtual rules. The airlock should include a short moment of real-world action (reach, touch, step) to re-establish the real rules.
4. **Long sessions confuse layers today.** Steinicke & Bruder's confusion between worlds happened with 2014 hardware. Better hardware will not make it rarer.
5. **Wait before driving.** Simulator guidance is decades old, specific, and directly reusable for the [re-entry checklist](../brainstorm/ideas-you.md#y4-re-entry-checklist).
