# Presence, time and memory

Three things a thread has to work against: the feeling of being *there*, the loss of real time, and memories that do not record which world they came from.

## Presence and breaks in presence

- **Slater (2009)** splits presence into two illusions: **place illusion**, the feeling of being in the place shown, and **plausibility illusion**, the feeling that what happens there is really happening. Together they make people respond realistically. On the way out, these two illusions are what has to be undone.
- **Slater & Steed (2000)** estimated presence by counting how often people reported switching from virtual to real during a session. Noticing that you are in VR behaves like a discrete event.
- **Garau et al. (2008)** studied these **breaks in presence** in interviews: they have many causes, vary in intensity, and people take different amounts of time to recover from them.

For a thread, a break in presence is both the enemy (the thread must not cause needless ones) and the tool (an exit is a deliberate, permanent break in presence).

## Time

**Mullen & Davidenko (2021)** found time compression in VR: an interval people judged as five minutes held 28.5% more real time in VR than on a monitor, though only when VR came first. Their **2025 follow-up** found the effect inconsistent and dependent on the size of the virtual environment.

The evidence that VR distorts time is real but not settled. Either way, the subjective clock inside is not reliable, which is the case for a clock anchored outside ([H4](../brainstorm/ideas-hardware.md#h4-reality-heartbeat), [P9](../brainstorm/ideas-platform.md#p9-rope-length)).

## Memory: which world did that happen in?

**Johnson & Raye (1981)** described *reality monitoring*: how the mind decides whether a memory came from perception or imagination, using cues such as sensory detail and context.

- **Hoffman et al. (2001)** had people touch real and virtual objects and tested their memory of the source a week later. People seem to reconstruct a memory's qualities from the source they decide it came from, rather than the other way round.
- **Bonnail et al. (2024)**, "Was it Real or Virtual?", confirmed that people already confuse VR and real memories with current headsets (N=29). Crucially, people **rely on cues such as imperfect rendering** to tell the two apart. As rendering improves, those cues disappear and confusion should grow.
- **Bonnail et al. (2023)** mapped how extended reality could deliberately alter users' memories, and the risks.
- **Cheymol & Lopes (2025)** (see [leaving VR](exiting-vr.md)) found people remembering the virtual location of an object in place of the real one, right after leaving.

## What this means for the thread

1. **Bonnail et al. (2024) is direct evidence for this book's central worry.** The cues people use to tell real from virtual are the [tells](../problem/why-harder.md#the-tells-are-breadcrumbs) that engineering is removing. As tells go, something else must carry the information.
2. **Memory is part of the trust chain, and it is already unreliable.** Records kept outside your head (the [black box](../brainstorm/ideas-hardware.md#h7-black-box), the [return stack](../brainstorm/ideas-platform.md#p2-the-return-stack)) matter even without any hostile simulation.
3. **An exit is a designed break in presence.** The research on breaks in presence (what causes them, how long recovery takes) is the research on how to build one well.
